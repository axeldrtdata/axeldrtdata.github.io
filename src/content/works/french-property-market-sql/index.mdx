## The question

Laplace Immo, a national network of estate agencies, wanted a proof of concept: a relational database of property sales that its teams could query to understand the market. The scope was the **first half of 2020**, a period that happens to include the first COVID-19 lockdown, from 17 March to 11 May.

My job was to design and build the database, then answer twelve business questions in SQL: how many apartments sold, where, at what price per square metre, and how the market reacted to the crisis.

## Building the database

Three public sources fed the database: the **DVF** files (Demandes de Valeurs Foncières), the official record of property sales with each property's type, surface, rooms, price and location; **INSEE** census data for the population of each commune; and the **data.gouv** geographic reference for the codes and names of regions, departments and communes.

Before building anything, I wrote a **data dictionary** listing every field with its type, length, nature and management rules, so that anyone could take the project over. I then designed a normalised schema of four tables linked by foreign keys, which avoids duplicating a commune's information on every sale.

On the **GDPR** side, the data is public and official, and only the fields needed for the analysis were kept. Directly identifying data was removed, and the database is backed up regularly, stored locally in a dedicated folder, with restricted access. I also prepared, from the design stage, a ready-to-use query for the right to erasure (article 17). It does not use cascading deletes, a mechanism that would automatically erase the linked rows in other tables: every deletion is written explicitly, to keep full control over what is erased and avoid any accidental deletion.

```sql
-- Right to erasure: delete one sale, then its property only if nothing else refers to it
DELETE FROM ventes WHERE id_vente = 'v001';

DELETE FROM biens
WHERE id_bien = 'b001'
  AND NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM ventes WHERE ventes.id_bien = 'b001');
```

Paris stands at over €12,000 per m², about three times the price recorded in the Rhône or Corse-du-Sud, and the top three are all in Île-de-France. Across all departments, the average price per m² ranges from under €700 in the Creuse to over €12,000 in Paris. The gap holds for larger homes too: an apartment of five rooms or more costs €8,750 per m² in Île-de-France, four times more than in Hauts-de-France. A house in Île-de-France, at about €4,000 per m², remains far more affordable than a Paris apartment.

Size also matters: a three-room apartment costs **12% less per m²** than a two-room one. Small surfaces command a premium, because demand for them is so strong in high-pressure areas.

## The market and the lockdown

```sql
WITH quarters AS (
    SELECT
        COUNT(CASE WHEN date BETWEEN '2020-01-01' AND '2020-03-31' THEN 1 END) AS sales_q1,
        COUNT(CASE WHEN date BETWEEN '2020-04-01' AND '2020-06-30' THEN 1 END) AS sales_q2
    FROM ventes
)
SELECT sales_q1, sales_q2,
       ROUND((sales_q2 - sales_q1) * 100.0 / sales_q1, 2) AS growth_pct
FROM quarters;
```

Sales went from 16,776 in the first quarter to 17,393 in the second: **+3.7%**, while the lockdown covered half of the second quarter. A collapse could have been expected. This slight rise rather reflects a catch-up effect: transactions frozen during the lockdown were completed as soon as restrictions lifted, in May and June.

<Takeaway label="Key finding">Île-de-France dominates both volumes and prices, and sales kept going through the first lockdown.</Takeaway>

## Local dynamics

At commune level, 48 communes recorded at least 50 sales in the first quarter, led by the 17th, 15th and 18th arrondissements of Paris; seven of the top ten are Paris arrondissements, alongside Nice, Bordeaux and Nantes.

Raw volume favours big cities, so the twelfth question asked for sales relative to population. Among communes of more than 10,000 inhabitants, the most active market per head is the **2nd arrondissement of Paris**, with 5.84 sales per 1,000 inhabitants. Three poles account for 17 of the top 20: central Paris (8 communes), the Mediterranean coast (5) and the Atlantic coast (4), two logics that combine dense urban investment and second homes by the sea.

To rank the communes within each department, I used a window function. `ROW_NUMBER()` gives each commune a rank, `ORDER BY` sorts them from the highest average sale value to the lowest, and `PARTITION BY` restarts the ranking at 1 for each department. Keeping ranks 1 to 3 then gives the top 3 of every department in a single query:

```sql
ROW_NUMBER() OVER (
    PARTITION BY biens.code_dep
    ORDER BY AVG(ventes.montant) DESC
) AS rank_in_department
```

The contrasts are sharp even between neighbours: in the Alpes-Maritimes, Saint-Jean-Cap-Ferrat leads with an average sale value of €969,000, almost three times that of Gignac-la-Nerthe, the top commune in the neighbouring Bouches-du-Rhône, at €330,000. In the Rhône, a peri-urban commune, Ville-sur-Jarnioux (€485,000), even outranks Lyon's most expensive arrondissements.

## Limits

- **One semester only**, and an unusual one: the lockdown makes it hard to separate a seasonal effect from a crisis effect, and DVF records the date of the notarial deed, signed two to three months after the agreement: second-quarter sales therefore mostly reflect agreements signed before the lockdown. The figures show these sales catching up, but not yet, or at least not fully, the lockdown's effect on new agreements.
- **Averages are sensitive to DVF outliers.** The most expensive apartment of the semester is recorded at €9 million for 9.1 m²: most likely a whole building sold in one deed but recorded against a single small unit. One such sale is enough to push up a commune's average price per m². A rule that sets aside extreme prices per m² would make every average more reliable.
- **The quarter-on-quarter comparison counts all property types**, houses included, while the rest of the analysis focuses mostly on apartments: the 3.7% rise does not describe the apartment market alone.

## What I learned

Writing the data dictionary and the schema before any query felt slow, but it made the twelve queries straightforward: I already knew which table held each piece of information, with which type, and through which key to join the tables. For the sales-per-inhabitant query, for instance, it was enough to follow the foreign keys, from sales to properties to communes, to reach the population. This project also taught me that a raw count always favours big cities: relating sales to population reveals other markets, like Paris's 2nd arrondissement, invisible in a simple ranking of sales.
