## The question

The FAO, the United Nations agency that leads the fight against hunger, wanted a first picture of world food security from its own data. The brief came as a list of questions: how many people are undernourished, how many people the food available could theoretically feed, how that food is used, which countries suffer most, and where food aid goes.

Behind the list sits one question: **is hunger a problem of production, or of distribution?**

## Four FAO files

| File | Content | Period | Countries |
| ---- | ------- | ------ | --------- |
| Population | Population per country, in thousands | 2013–2018 | 236 |
| Food balance sheets | For each country and product: calories, proteins and fats per person, production, trade and uses | 2017 | 174 |
| Food aid | Tonnes of aid received, by product | 2013–2016 | 76 |
| Undernourishment | Undernourished people, in millions, as three-year averages | 2012–2014 to 2017–2019 | 203 |

The preparation was mostly about making four files speak the same language. I converted every quantity to the same units and turned text values such as `<0.1` into numbers. The undernourishment file only gives three-year averages: for the year 2017, I used the average of 2016, 2017 and 2018, centred on 2017. The other files give yearly values directly.

## The world produces enough food

In 2017, **535.7 million people were undernourished**, 7.1% of the world population, or 1 person in 14.

To estimate how many people the world's food could feed, I multiplied each country's daily calories per person by its population and by 365, then divided the world total by an average need of 2,400 kcal per day, taken from an FAO source. The calories available in 2017 could feed **about 8.8 billion people**, 1.3 billion more than the world population that year. Plant products alone could feed 96% of it.

<Takeaway label="The first answer">The food exists: what fails is its path to the plate.</Takeaway>

Cereals tell the same story more sharply: people eat 43% of them directly, and **36% go to animal feed**. More than a third of the world's cereals are used to produce meat, eggs and dairy.

## An unequal table

The averages hide a wide gap between countries. In 29 of the 174 countries, the food available is below the daily need.

The ten best-supplied countries, six of them in Europe, have 3,625 kcal per person per day on average, 1,225 more than the need. The ten least-supplied, seven of them in Africa, have 2,060, a deficit of 340. In theory, the surplus of one person in the first group could close the gap of three to four people in the second.

Where undernourishment is measured, it reaches almost half the population in the hardest-hit countries.

Thailand shows how food can leave a country with hunger. In 2017, about 9% of Thais were undernourished, yet Thailand exported **25.2 million tonnes of cassava, or 83% of what it grew**. The data cannot say whether those exports reduced what Thais eat, but it shows a crop grown mainly for export, with a commercial logic that can override local food security.

## Where food aid goes

Between 2013 and 2016, Syria, Ethiopia and Yemen received the most food aid, ahead of South Sudan, Sudan and Kenya. Seven of the ten largest recipients are in Africa, the other three in Asia, and many of them were facing war or a major crisis. Aid followed acute crises: it fell sharply for Ethiopia, Sudan and South Sudan in 2015, while Yemen's rose again as its war escalated.

<Takeaway label="Key finding">Aid responds to acute crises rather than supporting the countries in need over time: a firefighting logic rather than prevention.</Takeaway>

## What I would do next

- **Use the full FAOSTAT series**, to follow the same countries over time instead of comparing a single year.
- **Complete the aid data with the World Food Programme's figures**, to cover the countries and years missing from the FAO data.
- **Weight the theoretical calculation by real needs**, which vary with age, sex and activity, rather than a single 2,400 kcal average.

## Limits

- **The undernourished total is a minimum:** countries without an estimate count as zero, and the ranking of rates only covers the countries that have one.
- **Food availability is not consumption:** the calories available include what is wasted in shops and at home. The 8.8 billion people are therefore a theoretical ceiling: the food actually eaten would feed fewer.
- **The theoretical calculation leaves aside** transport, storage, prices and politics, which are precisely what decides who eats.

## What I learned

Before calculating anything, I boiled the FAO's list of questions down to one: does hunger come from a lack of production or from a distribution problem? Every calculation then served to answer it, from how many people the available food could feed to where food aid goes. I also learned that a theoretical result depends on its assumptions: the 8.8 billion rest on an average need of 2,400 kcal a day, an assumption that should always sit next to the figure. Finally, a figure speaks louder at the scale of a person: saying that the surplus of one person in the best-supplied countries could close the gap of three to four people in the least-supplied ones makes the inequality more concrete than a gap in calories.
